Handling Objections

At this stage of the sales call, everything logical has already happened.

● You’ve run discovery

● You’ve identified the problem

● You’ve positioned your solution

● You’ve explained deliverables

● You’ve answered questions

● You’ve presented the price

Now the prospect says something that isn’t:

“Yes, let’s move forward.”

That statement - anything other than a clear yes - is an objection.

Common examples:

● “I need to think about it”

● “I need to talk to my partner”

● “It’s a lot of money”

● “The timing isn’t great right now”

Most salespeople hear these and immediately try to argue, convince, or push. That’s exactly why they lose the deal.

The Core Truth About Objections

Almost all objections are smokescreens

A smokescreen is a surface-level explanation that hides the real reason someone hasn’t committed yet.

This happens for two reasons:

1. They don’t want to offend you

Saying “I don’t trust this” or “I’m scared” feels uncomfortable, so they default to something socially acceptable.

2. They haven’t fully processed it themselves

Their brain reaches for the safest placeholder response:

“I need to think about it.”

Once you understand this, objection handling becomes much simpler.

Because beneath every smokescreen, there are only two real objections: The only real objections

1. Fear

2. Finances

Everything else - timing, partner, thinking, logistics - is just a wrapper around one of those two.

Your job is not to fight the wrapper.

Your job is to calm the conversation and uncover what’s actually going on.

The Objection Handling Loop

Every objection you handle follows the same repeatable loop:

1. Diffuse the objection

2. Get buy-in

3. Get double buy-in

4. Clarify the real objection

5. Isolate and dismantle the logic

6. Create emotional commitment

7. Return to the close

You may need to run this loop more than once on a call.

That’s normal. Skilled closers don’t rush, they guide.

Step 1: Diffuse the Objection

When a prospect gives an objection, your first response matters more than anything else. Your goal here is simple:

Lower emotional resistance.

You do this by agreeing and accepting, not pushing.

Example: “I need to think about it”

Wrong response:

● “What exactly do you need to think about?”

● “Usually when people say that, it means no…”

● “If everything made sense, why wait?”

These spike resistance immediately.

Correct response:

“Totally fair. No worries at all, you should take the time you need.”

This does two things:

● It shows confidence

● It removes pressure

The prospect relaxes. Their guard drops.

Only after that do you move forward.

Step 2: Get Buy-In

Once the objection is diffused, you need to establish whether they actually want the outcome you’re selling.

This is not about convincing.

It’s about clarity.

The Buy-In Question

“Before you go, can I ask, do you feel like this kind of editing support could actually help you achieve the results you want?”

This question matters because:

● If they say no, you now know the objection is about belief

● If they say yes, you’ve reopened the conversation legitimately

If they say “No” or “I’m not sure”

Don’t argue.

Instead, explore:

“Is there any specific reason you feel like it wouldn’t work for you?”

Now one of two things happens:

1. They give a real objection

→ You address it directly later in the framework

2. They say they’re unsure or can’t articulate why

→ That means fear is present, but unclear

Bring it back to logic and support:

“If you showed up consistently, followed the system, and had our team supporting you through the content process, do you feel like it could work?”

If they say yes, you move on.

If hesitation remains, ask:

“What’s actually coming up for you that’s making you unsure?”

This question surfaces the real objection.

Step 3: Get Double Buy-In

Once they’ve said yes, you’re not done.

You now need to lock in their internal justification.

The Double Buy-In Question

“What specifically about this do you feel would help you the most?”

or

“Which part of the process stands out as most valuable for where you’re at right now?” This is a critical moment.

When they answer, they are selling themselves:

● They verbalize value

● They connect the offer to their pain

● They reinforce their own decision-making logic

From this point forward, your role is no longer to persuade, it’s to guide them through whatever is blocking commitment.

Step 4: Clarify the Real Objection

Once the prospect has said something like:

● “I need to think about it”

● “I need to talk to my partner”

● “The timing isn’t great”

You already know these are smokescreens.

Now you calmly bring it into the open.

The Clarification Question

“When you say you need to {{objection}}, what’s actually coming up for you?” This question does three things:

● It removes ambiguity

● It invites honesty

● It reframes you as a helper, not a closer

Now listen carefully.

No matter what they say, it will fall into one of three buckets:

1. Money

2. Timing

3. Partner / external approval

Each of these maps back to fear or finances.

Let’s break them down one by one.

Handling the “It’s a Lot of Money” Objection If they say:

“It’s just a big investment.”

Do not justify your price.

Instead, shift the frame from cost to opportunity cost.

Reframe the Decision

“That makes sense. Can I ask, what do you feel not having consistent, high-quality content is costing your business right now?”

Then quantify it with them.

For example:

● Missed uploads

● Inconsistent growth

● Lost inbound leads

● Reduced leverage on past content

“If one strong piece of content brings in even a few thousand dollars in opportunity, missing that weekly compounds quickly.”

The logic becomes simple:

● The cost of inaction is greater than the cost of action

● The investment is not the risk, staying where they are is

Once framed correctly, money objections often soften immediately.

Handling the “Timing” Objection

If they say:

“It’s just not the right time.”

This is almost always a logic contradiction, not a real objection.

Earlier in discovery, they told you:

● They want to grow now

● They’re frustrated now

● They’re tired of inconsistency now

Your job is simply to remind them of their own words.

The Reminder Frame

“Earlier you mentioned that now feels like the moment you actually want to fix this, has something changed since then?”

People hate going back on themselves.

Most will say:

“No… not really.”

You’re not forcing them.

You’re helping them see the inconsistency.

Then calmly continue:

“That’s why I wanted to check, because waiting usually just means the same problems repeat.”

Handling the “I Need to Talk to My Partner” Objection This objection is common, and often mishandled.

First, never disrespect the partner.

Second, understand this is rarely actually about the partner.

Step 1: Get Partner Buy-In (Indirectly)

“When you talk to your partner about this, do you feel like they’d want you to succeed and scale this part of your business?”

They’ll say yes.

“Why do you feel that way?”

Now the partner is positioned as supportive, not blocking.

Step 2: Reveal the Real Objection

Next question:

“In the past, when you’ve brought opportunities like this to them, what’s usually been the main hesitation?”

This reveals the truth:

● Fear of risk

● Past failure

● Money uncertainty

Now you label it.

Call It Out Gently

“Would it be fair to say it’s less about asking them, and more about some uncertainty you’re feeling?”

or, if it’s money:

“Would it be fair to say it’s more about the investment itself than the conversation?” If they agree, you’re back in control.

Step 3: The Responsibility & Leadership Frame (When Needed) If they still defer responsibility, you move to leadership.

“Can I ask you something, if this business succeeds or stalls, whose responsibility is that?”

They’ll say it’s theirs.

“The wins, the frustrations, the stress, that’s all something you’ve carried, right?” Yes.

Then:

“And your partner supports you, but they’re not responsible for making operational decisions in your business, are they?”

This reframes the decision:

● Emotional support ≠ decision authority

● Responsibility follows expertise

You’re not isolating them, you’re empowering them.

Then you return to the close.

Step 5: The Fear Protocol

There are only three types of fear prospects experience in B2B service sales. Your first job is to identify which one you’re dealing with.

Fear Type 1: They Don’t Trust You (or Your Service)

This shows up as:

● “I just found out about you”

● “Do you have more reviews?”

● “Can I talk to one of your clients?”

This is rarely about information.

It’s about risk avoidance.

How to Handle It

You don’t overwhelm them with proof.

You reframe urgency and honesty.

“I hear you, but I don’t think you booked this call just to explore for fun. This isn’t a new problem for you. It’s been around for a while, right?”

Let them agree.

Then:

“So this isn’t really about discovery, it’s about deciding whether you want to fix it now or keep carrying it.”

You’re not dismissing trust.

You’re calling out decision avoidance.

Then you move forward.

Fear Type 2: They Don’t Trust Themselves

This is extremely common with creators and founders.

You’ll hear:

● “I’m really busy”

● “I don’t know if I’ll stay consistent”

● “I’ve never been good at this”

This is identity-level fear.

How to handle it:

You don’t reassure them.

You challenge them, respectfully.

“That makes sense. Life is always busy, now or six months from now, something will come up.”

Then shift to standards:

“The real question is: are you willing to hold yourself to the standard of the future you want?”

Then force a binary:

“Do you want to be the person who builds this even when things are busy, yes or no?” If they hesitate:

“You don’t have to change. But why are you choosing to?”

End with:

“Can I challenge you to draw the line in the sand right now?”

This works because it reframes:

● Fear → identity

● Doubt → choice

Fear Type 3: They’ve Been Burned Before

You’ll hear:

● “I’ve tried this before”

● “It didn’t work last time”

● “I always quit halfway”

This is experience-based fear.

How to handle it:

You don’t argue about the past.

You position it as the reason they’re here.

“That’s exactly why you’re here. Doing the same thing alone is what didn’t work, not the goal itself.”

Then:

“Your future doesn’t change unless you choose something different than what you’ve already tried.”

You’re not promising perfection.

You’re promising support through difficulty.

Then you move to commitment.

Step 6: The Money Clarification Framework Before you treat something as a money objection, you must classify it properly. The Clarifying Question

“In terms of the investment, is it that you have it and it feels uncomfortable, or that you don’t fully have it and need to figure something out?”

This splits fear from finance immediately.

If They Have The Money (But It Feels Scary)

This is fear, not finance.

Return to the Fear Protocol.

If They Don’t Fully Have the Money

Now you collaborate, without discounting your value.

Recommit Buy-In

“If the investment wasn’t a factor for a moment, would this be a clear yes?” If yes, proceed.

Reality Check Question

“If an extra $1,500 a month showed up right now, would you invest it into this, or spend it elsewhere?”

If they say they’d invest:

“Okay. Let’s look at making this an easier yes.”

Then ask:

“What could you realistically invest right now to get started?”

This turns the conversation from can’t afford → how do we make this work responsibly. You’re negotiating structure, not value.

Deposits: When They Still Need Time

If commitment is there but hesitation remains, take a deposit.

● 10% of the retainer

● Secures intent

● Drastically increases close rate

A deposit isn’t pressure.

It’s proof of seriousness.

Step 8: The Crossroad Questions

Once logic has been dismantled and fear addressed, you move to emotional commitment.

This is not manipulation.

This is alignment.

Crossroad Question 1

“Do you want the life and business where you’re consistently growing and providing for yourself and your family — yes or no?”

Crossroad Question 2

“Are you prepared to show up as the person who makes that happen, even when it’s uncomfortable, with our support?”

Crossroad Question 3

“Why are you ready to do this now?”

Let them answer.

Then close the loop:

“That version of you, the one with freedom and momentum, what does he do today to guarantee he ends up there?”

Pause.

Then:

“Let’s get you set up.”

The Full Objection Loop (Final Framework)

Students should now internalize this loop:

1. Diffuse

2. Buy-in

3. Double buy-in

4. Clarify the objection

5. Isolate faulty logic

6. Apply fear or money protocol

Key Takeaways

● Objections are not rejections

● Smokescreens protect fear

● Logic must be dismantled before emotion can commit

● Great closers guide, they don’t push

● Closing is a skill loop, not a moment

Action Steps

1. Memorize the objection loop, not scripts

2. Practice identifying fear vs finances

3. Rehearse clarification questions until natural

4. Never justify price, reframe value

5. Lead with calm certainty
